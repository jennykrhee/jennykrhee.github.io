Hi, I’m Jenny!

Digital technologies promise to transform healthcare, but their effects depend on how they are implemented within real organizations and regulatory environments. A question that motivates much of my research is why technological potential does not always translate into realized impact.

My current work explores this question in the contexts of telehealth and data regulation. More recently, I have begun exploring how AI is changing healthcare organizations and how its growing reliance on data is reshaping the generation, governance, and use of data. I am excited to continue developing this emerging stream of research.

Methodologically, I draw on causal inference and computational methods, including machine learning, large language models, and simulation.

I always enjoy exchanging ideas with researchers and practitioners, and I welcome questions, conversations, and new collaborations! Feel free to reach out at krhee1 [at] asu.edu.

Research

Technology-Mediated Services in Health Care: Evaluating Telehealth Delivery Models for Underserved Populations

Forthcoming, Information Systems Research

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Xiao Liu, Tan Lekwijit, Pei-yu Chen

WISE 2022 · INFORMS 2023

Evidence on telehealth’s effectiveness has been mixed, in part because telehealth encompasses distinct delivery models that address different barriers to care. We distinguish between two models: one that connects patients at clinics with external specialists, and another that connects patients at home with providers at the clinic. The first reduces search and verification frictions and is associated with greater access to mental health care, while the second reduces transportation and tracking frictions and is associated with better adherence to clinical protocols. Our findings highlight the importance of considering which telehealth model is implemented, the frictions it addresses, and the outcomes it is designed to improve.

More Virtual Care, More Pressure? The Impact of Centralized Telehealth Implementation on In-Person Provider Workload

Nominee, CIST 2026 Best Student Paper Award Manuscript in Preparation for Management Science

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Tan Lekwijit, Xiao Liu, Pei-yu Chen

CIST 2026 · INFORMS Healthcare 2026 · WISE 2024 · CHITA 2024

We study a health system that adopted centralized telehealth, in which a dedicated team provides virtual care while primary care providers continue to see patients in person. This model is designed to expand access while shielding in-person providers from the additional workload associated with virtual care. We examine whether this organizational separation achieves that goal by considering multiple dimensions of provider workload, including the volume, timing, and clinical complexity of demand. Leveraging randomized patient eligibility for the program, we find that greater exposure to centralized telehealth increases in-person visit volume, makes demand more variable, and shifts the patient mix toward greater clinical complexity. These findings show that centralized telehealth can reshape, rather than simply divert, the demand faced by in-person providers.

Data Regulation and the Microstructure of Clinical Trials

Finalist, 2026 INFORMS eBusiness Section Best Paper Award Job Market Paper Preparing for submission to Information Systems Research

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Marios Papachristou, Tian Lu, Pei-yu Chen

Presenting at INFORMS 2026

We examine how privacy regulation affects clinical trial design. Clinical trials generate evidence whose value can extend well beyond the sponsoring firm, while sponsors bear the costs of collecting and governing the underlying data. The GDPR increased these costs for trials involving European sites. Because European patient participation is not required for drug approval, sponsors retain discretion over whether to absorb the additional compliance costs or shift trial activity elsewhere, making regulatory exposure an organizational design consideration. Comparing sponsors with different levels of pre-GDPR exposure to Europe, we find little change in planned enrollment or core measures of trial success, but selective reductions in longer-term and supplementary measures and shifts in trial activity away from GDPR jurisdictions. These adjustments have downstream implications: trials conducted by more-exposed sponsors receive fewer citations from subsequent research, while geographic shifts move trials toward populations with which sponsors have greater prior experience. The findings suggest that firms preserve components of evidence generation that are more tightly constrained by regulatory requirements while adjusting along more flexible margins.
