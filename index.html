Hi, I’m Jenny!

Digital technologies promise to transform healthcare, but their effects depend on how they are implemented within real organizations and regulatory environments. A question that motivates much of my research is why technological potential does not always translate into realized impact.

My current work explores this question in the contexts of telehealth and data regulation. More recently, I have begun exploring how AI is changing healthcare organizations and how its growing reliance on data is reshaping the generation, governance, and use of data. I am excited to continue developing this emerging stream of research.

Methodologically, I draw on causal inference and computational methods, including machine learning, large language models, and simulation.

I always enjoy exchanging ideas with researchers and practitioners, and I welcome questions, conversations, and new collaborations! Feel free to reach out at krhee1 [at] asu.edu.

Technology-Mediated Services in Health Care: Evaluating Telehealth Delivery Models for Underserved Populations

Conditionally Accepted at Information Systems Research

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Xiao Liu, Tan Lekwijit, Pei-yu Chen

WISE 2022 · INFORMS 2023

This study examines how telehealth delivery models are associated with access to care and protocol adherence in underserved populations. While prior research reports mixed evidence on telehealth’s effectiveness, we argue that these inconsistencies arise because telehealth is not a single intervention but implemented through distinct service configurations that address different barriers. Drawing on the theoretical framework of digital economics, we conceptualize telehealth as a technology-mediated service that can alter key economic frictions, including search and verification, transportation, tracking, and setup costs. We focus on two widely adopted models—specialist-facilitated telehealth and remote telehealth—and theorize that each addresses different subsets of these frictions, leading to distinct effects on care access and protocol adherence across settings. Using panel data from U.S. Community Health Centers on mental health services, we document three findings. First, adoption of the specialist-facilitated model is associated with increased access to mental health care, particularly in areas with greater clinical need. Second, remote telehealth is not associated with increased access on average but is associated with higher adherence to standardized depression screening and follow-up protocols. These effects are consistent with improvements in screening and documentation processes rather than increases in utilization. Third, the estimated effects of both models vary across local cost environments. The access effects of specialist-facilitated telehealth are larger in areas where search and verification frictions are greater. In contrast, the effects of remote telehealth are attenuated where patient-side setup costs are high but are larger where transportation barriers and documentation frictions are greater. These findings advance a model-specific perspective on telehealth’s effects, extend the digital economics framework to technology-mediated services, and provide evidence that the estimated effects of digital health interventions vary with how delivery models interact with underlying cost environments.

More Virtual Care, More Pressure? The Impact of Centralized Telehealth Implementation on In-Person Provider Workload

Manuscript in Preparation for Management Science

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Tan Lekwijit, Xiao Liu, Pei-yu Chen

CIST 2026 · INFORMS Healthcare 2026 · WISE 2024 · CHITA 2024

Healthcare organizations are increasingly implementing centralized telehealth programs staffed by dedicated virtual care teams in an effort to alleviate pressure on in-person providers. In practice, however, introducing a virtual care entry point may change how care demand is generated and subsequently channeled to in-person providers. Prior research suggests that telehealth may increase care utilization, yet its operational impact on providers remains unclear, particularly in centralized telehealth settings where in-person providers do not participate in telehealth delivery. We study a large health system that introduced a centralized video visit program for primary care in which dedicated nurse practitioners conduct video visits while primary care providers (PCPs) continue to deliver only in-person care. Leveraging the program's randomized patient eligibility design, we exploit variation in telehealth eligibility within PCPs' patient panels to estimate the effect of telehealth on provider workload along three dimensions: volume, variability, and complexity. We find that greater telehealth eligibility within a PCP's patient panel increases the volume of in-person visits handled by that provider. Telehealth also alters the temporal structure of demand, increasing the frequency with which providers face workloads above their typical capacity and concentrating visits during already busy periods. Finally, telehealth shifts the composition of in-person care toward patients with greater comorbidity burden, although it does not increase visit-level service intensity. These findings show that centralized telehealth can increase both the amount and operational complexity of work faced by in-person providers and highlight the need to account for cross-channel workload spillovers in capacity planning and workforce management.

Data Regulation and the Microstructure of Clinical Trials

Job Market Paper Preparing for submission to Information Systems Research

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Marios Papachristou, Tian Lu, Pei-yu Chen

Presenting at INFORMS 2026

Privacy regulation strengthens individuals' control over personal data but can also increase the cost of generating the data on which this scientific evidence depends. While prior research has examined how privacy regulation affects innovative activity, much less is known about how it changes the process through which scientific evidence is produced. We conceptualize trial design as an evidence-generation strategy with required components that satisfy scientific and regulatory objectives and a discretionary margin over which sponsors retain flexibility. This framework predicts that organizations preserve comparatively constrained components of evidence generation while concentrating adjustment within components that offer greater design flexibility. Exploiting cross-sponsor variation in predetermined exposure to the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) among industry-sponsored Phase II clinical trials, we find that privacy regulation leaves planned enrollment and primary endpoints largely unchanged but selectively reduces longitudinal secondary endpoints and shifts the geographic composition of trials away from GDPR-related jurisdictions toward the alternative regions. To examine organizational responses beyond the observed GDPR regime, we develop a structural simulation of sponsors' joint trial design decisions. The simulation shows how informational and geographic adjustments jointly determine regulated data exposure and illustrates how the magnitude and structure of compliance costs can produce disproportionate compositional responses. Our findings identify trial design as an important organizational margin through which privacy regulation reshapes the scientific evidence generated by innovation. This study extends Information Systems research on privacy regulation and data governance by shifting attention from aggregate innovation outcomes to the microstructure of evidence generation, revealing how regulation reshapes the underlying process through which scientific knowledge is produced.%We contribute to the information systems literature by showing that data governance changes organizational evidence generation selectively, affecting the depth and scope of evidence rather than uniformly reducing data-intensive activity.
