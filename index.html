Hi, I’m Jenny!

Digital technologies promise to transform healthcare, but their effects depend on how they are implemented within real organizations and regulatory environments. A question that motivates much of my research is why technological potential does not always translate into realized impact.

My current work explores this question in the contexts of telehealth and data regulation. More recently, I have begun exploring how AI is changing healthcare organizations and how its growing reliance on data is reshaping the generation, governance, and use of data. I am excited to continue developing this emerging stream of research.

Methodologically, I draw on causal inference and computational methods, including machine learning, large language models, and simulation.

I always enjoy exchanging ideas with researchers and practitioners, and I welcome questions, conversations, and new collaborations! Feel free to reach out at krhee1 [at] asu.edu.

Research

Technology-Mediated Services in Health Care: Evaluating Telehealth Delivery Models for Underserved Populations

Forthcoming, Information Systems Research

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Xiao Liu, Tan Lekwijit, Pei-yu Chen

WISE 2022 · INFORMS 2023

This study starts from a puzzle: evidence on telehealth’s effectiveness is mixed. I argue that’s because telehealth isn’t a single intervention. I distinguish between two delivery models: one that connects patients at clinics with external specialists, and another that connects patients at home with their providers. The first relaxes search and verification frictions and is associated with greater access to mental health care; the second relaxes transportation and tracking frictions and is associated with better adherence to clinical protocols. So the question is not simply whether telehealth works, but which model works, for whom, and why.

More Virtual Care, More Pressure? The Impact of Centralized Telehealth Implementation on In-Person Provider Workload

Nominee, CIST 2026 Best Student Paper Award Manuscript in Preparation for Management Science

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Tan Lekwijit, Xiao Liu, Pei-yu Chen

CIST 2026 · INFORMS Healthcare 2026 · WISE 2024 · CHITA 2024

I study a health system that adopted centralized telehealth: a dedicated team provides virtual care, while primary care providers keep seeing patients in person. This design is meant to expand access while shielding in-person providers from additional burden. Whether it does, however, is unclear. For a provider, burden depends not just on how much demand arrives, but on when it arrives and who arrives. Exploiting random eligibility for the program, I find that instead of being shielded, in-person providers see more patients, face more volatile demand, and treat a sicker patient mix. In other words, telehealth doesn’t just take demand away from the clinic. It changes what’s left behind.

Data Regulation and the Microstructure of Clinical Trials

Finalist, 2026 INFORMS eBusiness Section Best Paper Award Job Market Paper Preparing for submission to Information Systems Research

Kyuyeon (Jenny) Rhee, Marios Papachristou, Tian Lu, Pei-yu Chen

Presenting at INFORMS 2026

This paper examines how privacy regulation affects clinical trial design. Clinical trials generate evidence whose value can extend well beyond the sponsoring firm, while sponsors bear the costs of collecting and governing that data. The GDPR increased those costs for trials with European sites. Because European patients are not required for drug approval, sponsors can either comply or bypass, and each option carries a different cost, making the response a business choice rather than a legal mandate. Comparing sponsors with different pre-GDPR exposure to Europe, I find little change in planned enrollment or core measures of trial success, but selective reductions in longer-term and supplementary measures and shifts in trial activity away from GDPR jurisdictions. These changes matter downstream: trials conducted by more-exposed sponsors receive fewer citations from subsequent research, while geographic shifts move trials toward populations sponsors had studied before. The results suggest that firms preserve the components of evidence generation that are more tightly constrained by regulatory requirements, while adjusting more flexible margins.
